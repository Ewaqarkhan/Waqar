Teaching Philosophy

I believe effective teaching in machine learning and cybersecurity must bridge the gap between mathematical formalism and engineering pragmatism. Students learn best when abstract concepts are immediately grounded in concrete, hands-on experiments — building a working intrusion detector, debugging a federated training loop, or interpreting a SHAP explanation on their own laptop. My approach combines three pillars:

  • Hands-on first. Every concept is paired with a runnable code artifact so students can poke at the model, break it, and rebuild it — internalizing the failure modes that matter in production.
  • Reproducibility as a habit. I teach version control, experiment tracking, and dataset documentation from day one, because trustworthy ML research is impossible without reproducible workflows.
  • Critical reading. Students practice dissecting recent papers — identifying assumptions, spotting evaluation gaps, and proposing follow-up experiments — so they can transition from consumers to producers of research.

Courses I am prepared to teach or co-teach include introductory and advanced machine learning, deep learning for time-series data, network security and intrusion detection, applied cryptography, and an applied research-methods seminar for senior undergraduates.
